# The Coaching Arc: A Synthesis of Methodology and Praxis

**Purpose:** The one document that explains how individual coaching actually works — from first contact to final session. Synthesizes evidence from 4 coaching engagements (Ruy + 3 Horacio case studies), the Axialent Coaching Handbook, and extended interviews with Horacio.

**Date:** February 11, 2026
**Status:** Doc A of the AI Coach Spec Plan. Pre-Phase C (Horacio interview will sharpen certain sections).

---

## 1. What Coaching Is (and Isn't)

### The Core Model

Individual coaching is a **development process, not a repair process.** The coach is an expert in *process*, not content — they help the coachee perceive clearly, make significant decisions, and implement them. The coachee is not broken; they have the capacity to grow.

> "Nuestro trabajo como coaches no es arreglar sino ayudar a crecer." — Axialent Coaching Handbook

### One Long Conversation

The most important framing: coaching is not a series of isolated sessions. It is **one long conversation distributed across six or more moments.**

> "Las sesiones no pueden ser tratadas como eventos aislados... es una larga conversación repartida en seis momentos." — Horacio

This means every session connects to the ones before and after it. The coach's job is to maintain the thread: tracking commitments, surfacing connections, remembering what the coachee said three sessions ago. The quality of this continuity is what separates competent coaching from transformative coaching.

### Shared Foundation with Team Effectiveness

Coaching draws from the same Conscious Business foundation as team effectiveness work:

- **BE → DO → HAVE** — Identity drives behavior drives results. Coaching works at all three levels but aims for the BE.
- **The Gap** — The distance between "who I am" and "who I want to be." This is the raw material of coaching.
- **Subject → Object** — Making invisible patterns visible. Coaching turns things the coachee is *subject to* (reactive patterns) into things they can *see* (and then choose).
- **Real Content Creates Real Learning** — Coaching uses the coachee's actual situations, not hypothetical exercises.
- **Trust Before Task** — The coaching relationship must be safe before it can be challenging.

What's coaching-specific (not shared with team work): the 1:1 relationship, the diagnostic debrief, the commitment cycle, the inter-session architecture, and the coach's private tracking.

## 2. Meeting Zero: Where the Arc Begins

### What All Setups Share

The formal methodology prescribes a tripartite "Meeting Zero" — coach, coachee, and boss together setting objectives. In practice, across all four studied engagements, Meeting Zero takes a different form: a **dyadic session (coach + coachee only)** without the boss present. The function, however, is universal: **initiate the relationship, surface the gaps, and plant the seeds for the entire engagement.**

Every observed setup, regardless of format, produces two outputs:
1. **A working gap inventory** — the raw material the coaching engagement will work on
2. **A coach's private hypothesis** — the diagnostic framing that guides the arc

How these outputs are generated differs based on whether a structured assessment (LSI) is available.

### Path A: The LSI Debrief (Corporate Cases)

In the three corporate engagements (CASO 1-3, each 6+LSI sessions), Meeting Zero centers on the **LSI diagnostic debrief** — a ~2-hour session combining relationship initiation, diagnostic review, and gap identification. The LSI data serves as the external mirror, especially LSI2 (others' perception), which grounds the coaching direction in something beyond the coachee's self-narrative.

This is not a deficiency over the prescribed tripartite meeting. The combined session is more powerful because the coachee *experiences* the gap through their own data rather than hearing it from their boss.

**The 5-Phase Anatomy:**

All three corporate cases follow the same debrief structure:

**Phase 1: Biographical Anchoring.** The coach elicits extensive career and personal history. This is not small talk — it's diagnostic data. CASO 3's "me topé con la pared, aprendí a la brava" (first leadership crisis at 29) directly explains the defensive patterns visible in the LSI. The biography becomes a lens the coach uses for the entire engagement.

**Phase 2: Pre-Diagnostic Self-Assessment.** Before or during the debrief, the coachee articulates their own development needs. CASO 3's coachee explicitly listed two areas ("firmeza" and "organización") before seeing any data. This creates a compare-and-contrast baseline: where does self-perception align with or diverge from the instrument?

**Phase 3: Item-by-Item LSI Exploration.** The longest phase (~1.5 hours). The coach walks through individual items, not aggregate scores, turning each into a conversation starter. The coachee connects abstract survey language to concrete behaviors:

> CASO 1: "Hizo una primera interpretación del item 'Dice lo que se espera' en el sentido de 'dice lo que piensa'. Le mostré que 'dice lo que piensa' podría entenderse como rojo más que como verde. Finalmente, hizo click cuando reconoció que en las juntas tiende a medir mucho sus palabras."

The coach re-explains the instrument from zero even when the coachee has pre-read the report — quality control over interpretation. The coach also selects "perlitas" (gems) to highlight proactively, and creates space for the coachee to "double click" on items that resonate.

**Phase 4: Coach's Private Diagnostic Hypothesis.** During the debrief, the coach formulates a private framing of the core challenge — not shared with the coachee, but guiding the entire arc:

> CASO 2: "[Nota: hay en esta expresión algo que todavía veo que razona en un nivel muy instrumental]" — the coach identifies the DO/BE gap
> CASO 3: "Será fundamental ayudarlo a salir del dilema binario entre hacer lo que el otro espera o hacer lo que yo quiero sin que el otro me importe."

These hypotheses, formed during the debrief, prove remarkably accurate across the full 6-session engagements.

**Phase 5: Working Themes Emerge.** The debrief closes with 2-3 identified focus areas and a declared working direction. These become the initial "conversational pantry" — a rich store of items, reactions, and surprises that both coach and coachee return to in future sessions.

**Surprise as Engagement Driver.** The most productive debrief moments occur when data challenges self-perception. All three cases document surprise as the emotional catalyst that makes the debrief stick:

> CASO 1: "Le sorprendió lo alto que salió Dependiente. No se siente así."
> CASO 3: "Sorprendido por la diferencia de percepción entre otros y él mismo."

### Path B: Gap Exploration Without Diagnostic (Ruy's Case)

When no structured assessment is available — or the coachee has already completed one in a different context — Meeting Zero takes a different form: **pure conversational exploration of needs.** Ruy's Dec 17, 2025 setup illustrates this path.

**Context:** Ruy initiated the coaching himself (asked HR), had already completed LSI and CB Assessment through building the Stoic platform, and had a pre-existing working relationship with Horacio. The session was ~30 minutes — shorter than the LSI debrief (~2 hours) — and had no boss/sponsor present.

**How it unfolded:**

1. **Light process orientation.** Horacio gave a brief explanation of the coaching process and explicitly discussed confidentiality. The lightness reflected the existing relationship — Ruy already had familiarity with the methodology.

2. **Open need exploration.** Horacio asked what Ruy needed coaching on. The presenting request: *understanding how to better lead his team and how they fit together.* Horacio "double-clicked" — explored what it meant, probed the underlying gaps.

3. **Gap list co-creation.** Through conversation, they co-created a list of 8 topics. Horacio wrote these down during the session and gave the written list to Ruy. The 8 gaps, in Ruy's words from Horacio's notes:

   1. *Este equipo comercial funciona* — The commercial team just works
   2. *Conectar comercial con producto* — Connect commercial with product
   3. *Hacer lo necesario para que el producto funcione* — Do what's needed to make the product work
   4. *Más usuarios para recibir más feedback sobre mi producto* — More users for more product feedback
   5. *Más tiempo para platicar con el equipo y rebotar ideas* — More time to talk with the team and bounce ideas
   6. *Continuar* — Continue (momentum/persistence)
   7. *Contar con una oficina para poder ir de manera presencial* — Have an office to be there in person
   8. *Conversaciones estratégicas efectivas. ¿Cómo sabemos que lo que estamos haciendo es lo adecuado?* — Effective strategic conversations. How do we know what we're doing is right?

   The topics span operational (#3, #4), relational (#2, #5, #7), and strategic (#8). They are interconnected — not a flat list but a web of related concerns. Topic #8 (strategic conversations) became the primary focus in Session 1, the most abstract and identity-level gap on the list.

4. **The Magic Wand technique.** Several gaps were not immediately articulable. When Ruy knew something was missing but couldn't name it (e.g., "the commercial team just works" — what's the gap?), Horacio used **ideal-scenario questioning**: "If you could have anything, what would it look like?" This converted vague desires into concrete gap statements. The technique serves the same function the LSI serves in Path A — surfacing gaps the coachee can't spontaneously articulate — but through conversation rather than instrument.

5. **No homework.** Clean handoff — no preparation requested between setup and Session 1.

**What was NOT present** (deviations from standard setup): no tripartite meeting, no boss expectations, no formal diagnostic, no detailed process walkthrough, no organizationally mandated goals. The engagement was entirely coachee-initiated and coachee-directed.

### Both Paths Converge: The Conversational Pantry

Regardless of path, Meeting Zero produces a **conversational pantry** — a rich store of items, reactions, and surprises that both coach and coachee return to in future sessions. The LSI pantry is data-driven (unexplored items, surprising scores). The gap-list pantry is desire-driven (unexplored topics, vague aspirations).

In both cases, the pantry is not consumed in the setup — it's **planted as seed material:**

> CASO 1, Session 2: "Volvió entonces sobre el LSI y la sorpresa que le causó que el Dependiente saliera tan alto."
> CASO 2, Session 1: "Inició la sesión reflexionando sobre el 'error de comunicación hacia afuera' que revelaba la brecha entre LSI 1 y LSI 2."
> Ruy, Session 1: Horacio read back all 8 items from the gap list, asking for validation and selection.

When a coachee arrives at a later session without a prepared topic, the unexplored pantry items become the default re-entry point.

### Connection to Session 1

The setup-to-Session-1 transition follows the same grammar in both paths:

```
Meeting Zero:  Surface gaps → create inventory → plant the pantry
Session 1:     "Here's what you said" → validate → "Which path?" → deep work
```

In the LSI path, Session 1 often continues the diagnostic thread directly (CASO 2 opened reflecting on the LSI1-LSI2 divergence). In the gap-list path, Session 1 opens with the coach reading back the list and asking the coachee to choose (Ruy chose #8 — strategic conversations). In both cases, Session 1 front-loads memory — demonstrating recall as a trust signal and establishing the engagement's thread.

The items NOT chosen in Session 1 don't disappear. They become yellow lights — tracked by the coach, available as re-entry points when current themes resolve or when the coachee arrives without an agenda.

## 3. The Session Arc

### 3.1 Session 1 — Opening the Relationship

Session 1 has a unique character. The coach opens by reading back the coachee's own words from Meeting Zero — whether LSI items and reactions, or (in Ruy's case) the gap list. This "reconnect" move serves as both memory signal ("I remember you") and structured choice ("Which of these do you want to work on?").

**The opening grammar for Session 1:**
```
"Here's what you said" → validate → "Which path?" → deep work → commit
```

In CASO 2, Session 1 directly continues the LSI gap: the coachee opens by reflecting on the "communication error" revealed by the LSI1-LSI2 divergence. In CASO 1, Session 1 opens with a completely new topic (organizational role change) — the coachee arrives with a live challenge that takes precedence over debrief themes. Both patterns are valid: the coach follows the coachee's energy while tracking the debrief material as a background resource.

What makes Session 1 distinctive is that the **coach front-loads memory.** Ruy's Session 1 spent ~15 minutes on recall (reading all 7 items from setup). This investment builds trust ("you're not starting over every session") and establishes the engagement's thread.

### 3.2 Sessions 2-4 — The Middle

#### Commitment as Doorway

From Session 2 onward, the session opening changes fundamentally. Instead of broad exploration, the coach opens through the prior session's commitment:

```
"What happened with X?" → report → extract learning → new work → new commit
```

This is the "Commitment as Doorway" principle — sessions don't open with "how are you?" but with "what did you do?" The commitment creates the thread between sessions. Cross-case evidence shows that **sessions following a commitment-free session are consistently weaker:**

| Case | Sessions with commitment | Correlation with opening quality |
|------|------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Ruy | 4/4 (100%) | All openings strong |
| CASO 1 | 4/6 (67%) | CS#3-4 (post-no-commitment) weakest |
| CASO 2 | 4/6 (67%) | CS#3-4 are the "drift" sessions |
| CASO 3 | 5/6 (83%) | All openings except CS#2 have threads |

#### The Commitment Cycle

The cycle is not simply "commit → do → report." It has internal structure:

```
Commit (end of session N)
    ↓
Act (between sessions)
    ↓
Report (opening of session N+1)
    ├── If completed:     Extract → "Was this luck or design?" → Systematize
    ├── If re-sequenced:  Track logic → Validate adjustment → Check original
    ├── If not done:      Non-judgmental inquiry → Diagnose (avoidance vs obstacle)
    └── If exceeded:      Celebrate briefly → Challenge → Push to next level
```

Commitments also escalate in type across the engagement:

| Session Position | Commitment Type | Level |
|-----------------|----------------|-------|
| Early (S1-2) | Behavioral: do a specific task | DO |
| Mid (S2-3) | Operational: design a system | DO/HAVE |
| Late (S3-4) | Reflective: examine a pattern | BE |
| Closing (S4+) | Declarative: accept an identity | BE (identity) |

This mirrors the BE→DO→HAVE model running in reverse: the coaching process naturally deepens the level at which commitments operate.

#### The Session 3 Emergence Pattern

One of the strongest cross-case findings: **around session 3, surface issues resolve and deeper root causes emerge.** This was confirmed in 3 of 4 cases:

| Case | What Emerged at ~Session 3 | How |
|------|---------------------------|-----|
| Ruy | Leadership identity (not owning the role) | Side comment caught by coach |
| CASO 1 | Family pattern behind catastrophic thinking | Deep exploration |
| CASO 2 | Procrastination as self-management | CS#4, after CS#3 drift |
| CASO 3 | COLEGA1 assertiveness challenge | Final minutes of CS#3, after a "bache" |

The pattern: surface issues require ~2-3 sessions to handle. Once the tactical layer is addressed, psychological space opens for deeper material. The emergence often happens **laterally** — through a side comment, a dead spot, or an unplanned topic — rather than through direct questioning.

#### The Vacuum/Bache as Productive Space

When the coachee arrives "empty" or a session hits a dead spot, this is not failure. Across 3 of 4 cases, these "baches" (dead spots) immediately preceded deeper theme emergence:

> CASO 3, Session 3: After reporting progress and hitting a gap with nothing left to say, COACHEE3 "finalmente decidió comentar algo" — the COLEGA1 situation that became the engagement's deepest work.

The productive emptiness principle: **resist filling silence.** The vacuum creates space for what the coachee isn't yet ready to bring voluntarily.

#### Mid-Engagement Drift

Around sessions 3-4 of a 6-session engagement, a predictable pattern appears: initial momentum is consumed, initial topics feel handled, but deeper work hasn't crystallized. This manifests as lower-energy sessions, coachees arriving without prepared topics, and conversations that feel circular.

Recovery techniques observed: coach-initiated recap of yellow lights, return to unexplored LSI items, reflective commitments that reframe the work's direction.

#### Theme Graduation

Themes don't remain static across an engagement. They **graduate** — moving from surface to root cause:

```
Surface problem → Work on it → Partial resolution → Space opens →
Deeper pattern becomes visible → Work on THAT → Identity-level resolution
```

Ruy's engagement shows the cleanest version: "missing strategic conversations" (surface) → "not owning the leadership role" (root). CASO 2 shows a horizontal variant: the theme shifts laterally (feedback → motivation → procrastination) rather than deepening vertically. CASO 3 shows recursion: the defensive pattern resolves, then reasserts in a new context.

### 3.3 The Final Session — Closing

The final session has a distinct grammar: **consolidate, don't expand.**

**Opening:** The coach arrives with a prepared synthesis spanning all sessions. In Ruy's case, Horacio opened with a four-theme overview connecting the entire arc. In the case studies, the opening is a recap invitation: "Let's look at what we covered."

**The closing anatomy** (from Ruy's Session 4, the most detailed transcript):

1. **Convergence synthesis** — Coach connects threads across all sessions, showing the coachee the arc they've traversed
2. **Emotional check** — "How do you feel about where you are?"
3. **Unresolved items acknowledged** — What remains open is named, not hidden
4. **Gift question** — A personalized self-coaching question left as a continued tool (observed in Ruy's case: "Where else in your life are you not owning the role?")
5. **Second season framing** — The engagement ending is a chapter boundary, not a story ending

Cross-case closing feedback consistently highlights two values: (1) the safe space — "nunca ni con nadie más tiene espacio para hablar de estas cosas" (CASO 1); (2) increased self-awareness — "se volvió más consciente de sus formas de pensar y actuar" (CASO 3).

**Resolution depth varies.** Only Ruy's case reached full identity-level resolution. The 6-session cases show the normal range: skills are learned, behaviors change, but deep identity work often remains incomplete. This is not failure — it is the realistic coaching outcome. The coach should name what remains, not pretend everything resolved.

## 4. The Connective Tissue

### Between-Session Rhythm

The invisible work that makes the "one long conversation" possible:

**Coach preparation (15 min pre-session):** "Leo rápidamente cómo fue fluyendo la cosa" — not every detail, but the flow and trajectory. The coach re-reads prior session notes and yellow lights. By Session 2+, the coach arrives with a hypothesis about what to check.

**Note-taking practice:**
- *During session:* Detailed notes while coaching (informing the coachee when taking notes — ICF principle)
- *Post-session:* 20-30 minutes for cleanup. Word document per coachee, chronological. Includes personal notes/reminders.

**Yellow lights:** The coach's private flags interspersed in session notes — topics to surface later, patterns to watch, concerns to track:

> "Veo riesgo de que se pierda en generalidades"
> "Ver cuánto de esto se puede relacionar con el dependiente de su LSI1"
> "Tener en cuenta indagar a fondo su preocupación sobre las malinterpretaciones"

Yellow lights are the coach's predictive layer. They guide topic selection, intervention timing, and the detection of avoidance patterns.

### Named Entity Tracking

People and situations mentioned by the coachee are tracked by name across all sessions. If the coachee mentions "Julián" in session 3, the coach recalls "Julián from session 2 who..." This creates the connection-making that signals deep listening:

> "This reminds me of what happened with Ivan — what's similar? What's different?"

In the case studies, named entities form a recurring cast: Ivan and Ignacio (CASO 1), Martín and Alberto (CASO 2), COLEGA1 and JEFE (CASO 3). The coach tracks not just names but relationship dynamics, commitments involving them, and unresolved situations.

### How Themes Deepen

The theory-practice pattern for theme evolution across sessions:

| Mechanism | What Happens | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Commitment follow-through** | Action between sessions produces data that deepens understanding | Ruy's Oseas conversation succeeded → revealed deeper question about role |
| **Vocabulary continuity** | Coach uses the coachee's own language from prior sessions | Horacio quotes Ruy's words back to him, creating recognition |
| **Side comment catching** | Coach notices incidental remarks that reveal deeper patterns | "That comment was almost offhand, but I see richness in it" |
| **Diagnostic re-entry** | LSI items resurface when surface issues clear | CASO 1 returns to Dependiente when no live challenge is pressing |
| **Mirror cases** | Structurally similar situations recur across the arc | CASO 1: Ivan (CS#1) → Ignacio (CS#5) — same feedback challenge, different person |

## 5. The Coach's Craft

### Role Shifts Across the Arc

The coach's role is not fixed — it adapts to what the coachee needs at each session position:

```
Meeting Zero:     Diagnostician / Interpreter
Sessions 1-2:    Navigator / Architect's assistant
Sessions 2-3:    Depth explorer / Space holder
Sessions 4-5:    Challenger / Skill teacher
Final Session:   Synthesizer / Truth-teller
```

The consistent pattern: Diagnostician → Explorer → Challenger → Synthesizer. But the specific role within each position varies by coachee. CASO 3's coach became a modeler (demonstrating reclamo through role play). CASO 2's coach became a storyteller (self-disclosing to create identification). The repertoire adapts to the coachee.

### Theory Meets Practice: The 8-Step Process

The Axialent Coaching Handbook prescribes 8 steps per session: Preparation → Opening → Understanding → Exploration → Decision → Role Play → Commitment → Closing.

In practice:

- **Steps 1-3 (Preparation, Opening, Understanding)** are always present but compressed. The 15-minute pre-session review is the real preparation. The opening adapts by session position (not a generic "how are you?").
- **Steps 4-5 (Exploration, Decision)** are the core of every session and take 60-80% of the time.
- **Step 6 (Role Play)** is rare — used in only 1 of 4 cases, and only when the coachee needed to learn a behavior they'd literally never performed. When used, Horacio adapted the technique (Modified Direct Version, not the standard handbook form).
- **Step 7 (Commitment)** is present in ~75% of sessions. When absent, the following session's opening is weaker. The commitment is non-negotiable in good practice.
- **Step 8 (Closing)** is brief — scheduling the next session and a warm goodbye.

The biggest theory-practice divergence: the handbook assumes each session is relatively standalone (coachee brings a new situation). In practice, sessions are deeply interconnected — the opening references prior sessions, commitments carry, and themes graduate. The 8-step process operates within a larger inter-session architecture that the handbook doesn't document.

### Intervention Repertoire

Techniques observed across 4 engagements, deployed based on context:

| Intervention | When Used | Example |
|-------------|-----------|---------|
| **Reframing** | Coachee is stuck in binary thinking | "Salir de la lógica binaria" (CASO 1 — work vs. health as false dilemma) |
| **"Hay que" → "Voy a"** | Coachee hedges at commitment time | "Te propongo que transformemos el 'hay que' en 'voy a'" (Ruy S2) |
| **Outlier vs. Design** | Coachee reports success | "Was this luck or intentional?" (Ruy S2) |
| **Role Play (Modified Direct)** | Coachee has never performed the behavior | Coach models reclamo using coachee's actual entities (CASO 3) |
| **Self-disclosure** | Coachee needs normalization | Coach shares own parallel experience to create identification (CASO 2) |
| **Cognitive deconstruction** | Coachee has catastrophic thinking pattern | "¿Qué es lo peor que puede pasar? ¿Qué probabilidad veo?" (CASO 1) |
| **Desdoblamiento** | Coachee needs to see themselves from outside | Third-person self-observation exercise (Ruy S3) |
| **ITC (Immunity to Change)** | Coachee's behavior contradicts their stated goals | Big assumption identification + low-risk experiment (CASO 2) |
| **Gift question** | Engagement closing | Personalized self-coaching question as parting tool (Ruy S4) |
| **Microteaching** | Specific skill gap identified | Short theory input + guided practice (CASO 2 — commitment cycle; CASO 3 — reclamo) |

**The justify-exclusion logic:** Interventions are opt-OUT, not opt-IN. The coach's default is to listen, explore, and question. Teaching, modeling, and reframing are deployed when the coachee is stuck, not as routine practice. Role play was used in 1 of 4 cases. Self-disclosure in 1 of 4. ITC in 1 of 4. The most common "intervention" is simply holding the space and tracking the thread.

### Four Coachee Types Observed

| Type | Characteristics | What Works | What Doesn't |
|------|----------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Identity Seeker** (Ruy) | Self-directed, high metacognitive capacity | Direct questioning, convergence synthesis, gift question | Role play (too performative for the issue) |
| **Safe Space User** (CASO 1) | Values the container itself, substitutes behavioral for cognitive work | Patience, metaphors, long-term relationship | Forced depth work, cognitive deconstruction |
| **Instrumental Reasoner** (CASO 2) | Approaches development as engineering | Frameworks (ITC, Daniel Pink), concrete examples, self-disclosure | Abstract self-work, sessions without goals |
| **Defensive Practitioner** (CASO 3) | Conflict-avoidant, capable but passive | Role play/modeling, explicit skill teaching, naming patterns | Silence-only approaches, abstract frameworks |

These types are suggestive, not definitive (4 cases only). But they illustrate that the coach's repertoire must adapt to the coachee — the same intervention can succeed with one type and fail with another.

## 6. What We Don't Know Yet

### Gaps Pending Phase C (Horacio Interview)

1. **The tripartite element:** Does a boss meeting happen separately before the debrief? When it does, what specifically occurs? How do boss expectations influence the coaching direction?

2. **Closing details:** The case notes for sessions 5-6 are brief. How does Horacio design the closing? What artifacts does he produce? What does he report to stakeholders and how does he protect confidentiality?

3. **The debrief as coaching:** Is the combined debrief (relationship + diagnostic + gap) intentional, or does it emerge naturally? Does Horacio design the debrief as the first coaching intervention?

4. **When things go wrong:** What happens when a coachee resists? When the engagement stalls? When the boss has conflicting expectations? The cases all show relatively cooperative coachees.

### Structural Unknowns

5. **Multi-engagement dynamics:** What happens in a "second season"? How does the coach re-enter after a gap? Does the arc repeat or build?

6. **Resolution depth vs. satisfaction:** All four coachees reported high satisfaction. But resolution depth varied dramatically (identity-level in Ruy's case, behavioral-only in CASO 1). How should coaching measure success?

7. **Quantitative diagnostics:** The LSI provides quantitative data. How does a pre-post comparison work? Does Horacio re-administer the LSI?

8. **Team context injection:** When coaching occurs within a team effectiveness program, how does team data enter the 1:1 space? How does confidentiality work?
